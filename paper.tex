\begin{abstract}
% =====================================================================
Vernacular earthen buildings make up a large share of the
built stock in sub-Saharan Africa, yet their hygrothermal behavior under
IoT monitoring remains largely unquantified. This paper presents an
end-to-end open-source pipeline, from raw sensor data to probabilistic
machine-learning predictions, applied to the Ecological and Digital
Building (EDB) of the École Polytechnique de Thiès, Senegal. The building
envelope combines compressed-earth blocks (CEB) and \textit{typha}
bio-based insulation. A total of 2,237 measurements at a nominal
10-minute interval, collected over two windows in July--August and
November--December 2025 spanning 41.8~days at 37\,\% completeness, are
consolidated, enriched with ERA5 reanalysis data via the Open-Meteo API,
and processed through six modular pipeline layers. Three convergent indicators quantify the
envelope thermal inertia: a damping ratio \sigrat of 0.54, a median
peak-temperature lag of $+1.75$\,h between exterior and interior, and a
52.5\,\% reduction in diurnal amplitude. Interior conditions satisfy the
ASHRAE~55 tropical comfort zone 67\,\% of the time in the dry season
versus only 17\,\% during the rainy season. A HistGradientBoosting
quantile model predicting temperature increments from 36 engineered
features, including CEB-aware lagged variables and Open-Meteo exogenous
inputs, achieves a mean absolute error of 0.09\,\textdegree{}C at
15\,min and 0.23\,\textdegree{}C at 1\,h, improving on persistence by 22.9\,\% (95\,\% CI [17.5, 27.8]) and
47.7\,\% ([43.2, 51.7]) respectively, against a linear autoregressive
and a seasonal naive baseline.
Averaged over the walk-forward test folds, permutation importance is
led by the hour-of-day cycle and places exterior temperature six hours
earlier first among exterior predictors; removing the 6-hour rolling
mean of exterior temperature costs 7.9\,\% of MAE, consistent with a
thermal memory of several hours. Exterior
moisture variables contribute little: ablation puts their combined
worth at 3.4\,\% of MAE, far below what hygroscopic coupling would
imply. Code and data that
reproduce every table and figure are available at \repourl.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

The buildings sector accounted for 32\,\% of global final energy use
and 34\,\% of energy-related CO$_2$ emissions in 2023~\cite{gsr2025},
with space cooling its fastest-growing end-use~\cite{iea2018cooling}.
In sub-Saharan Africa, vernacular construction, particularly
compressed-earth blocks (CEB) combined with locally harvested bio-based
insulation, offers a low-carbon alternative to imported cement-based
construction, but remains under-instrumented and poorly characterized
in the literature~\cite{morel2001}.

Low-cost IoT sensing and open reanalysis data (ERA5 via
Open-Meteo~\cite{zippenfenig2023}) now make it feasible to monitor such
buildings continuously and to complement sparse in-situ measurements
with physically consistent exogenous variables. Yet, to the best of our
knowledge, no published work has combined (i)~IoT-derived
characterization of CEB thermal inertia, (ii)~probabilistic short-term
prediction from sensor and reanalysis inputs, and (iii)~a reproducible
open-source pipeline, for a vernacular earthen building in a West
African tropical-sahelian climate.

The paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textit{six-layer open-source pipeline} (consolidation,
  Open-Meteo enrichment, preprocessing, CEB-aware feature engineering,
  quantile gradient boosting, visualization) running end-to-end from
  raw CSV sensor exports to publication-ready diagnostics and
  probabilistic forecasts.

\item A \textit{quantitative thermal signature} of the \ceb envelope,
  derived from 2,237 measurements and three metrics (damping ratio,
  peak lag, amplitude reduction), measured at building scale rather
  than on material specimens.

\item A \textit{data-driven indication of envelope thermal memory} from
  feature importance and ablation: at $h{+}1$\,h, exterior temperature
  six hours earlier is the leading exterior predictor of interior
  temperature and the first predictor of all in the rainy season, and
  removing the 6-hour rolling mean of exterior temperature costs
  $+7.9\,\%$ MAE. The envelope responds to outdoor conditions over
  several hours, a time scale recovered without physical supervision.
\end{enumerate}

The case study is the Ecological and Digital Building (EDB) of the
École Polytechnique de Thiès in Senegal, built with CEB walls and \textit{typha}
insulation under PNEEB-Typha, the Senegalese national programme for
energy efficiency in buildings, whose Typha component develops
bio-based insulation from the invasive reed \textit{Typha
australis}~\cite{niang2018}. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the
site and its two seasonal regimes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================


\textit{IoT monitoring of earthen and vernacular buildings.}
Compressed-earth construction has been studied for its thermal mass and
hygroscopic properties~\cite{morel2001,liuzzi2018}, and wall-level
transfer models exist for rammed earth~\cite{soudani2017}. Continuous
IoT monitoring of complete vernacular buildings under real occupancy
and real climate remains sparse, particularly in West Africa, where
studies address static characterization of individual CEB
specimens~\cite{fabbri2014} or qualitative comfort surveys without
sensor data. The present work provides a longitudinal series spanning
two seasonal regimes.

\textit{Data-driven thermal prediction for buildings.}
Machine learning is widely applied to building energy forecasting in
well-instrumented settings, from classical time-series
methods~\cite{deb2017} to deep architectures~\cite{li2021}. Depth is
not always required: on windowed forecasting problems a properly
engineered gradient-boosting model matches or outperforms deep networks
across nine datasets~\cite{elsayed2021} while remaining far cheaper to
fit. Yet most published models assume multi-zone sensor networks,
multi-year records and mild seasonal transitions. The low-data
single-sensor regime studied here (one node, two discontinuous windows,
a single building) has received little attention for sub-Saharan
earthen buildings, and it is the regime most African research contexts
work in.

\textit{ERA5 reanalysis as exogenous input.}
ERA5-Land~\cite{muñozsabater2021} provides physically consistent
gridded reconstructions of past atmospheric conditions at
0.1\textdegree{} and hourly resolution, freely accessible via the
Open-Meteo API~\cite{zippenfenig2023}. Its use as an exogenous model
input is established for solar forecasting and agriculture, but its
integration into building-scale thermal prediction has not, to our
knowledge, been reported for vernacular African buildings. Enrichment
is feasible here (MAE vs.\ on-site sensor
${\approx}1.0$--$1.5$\,\textdegree{}C, $r>0.9$) and enables a feature
importance analysis impossible with a single on-site variable,
including its negative verdict on exogenous moisture
(Section~\ref{ssec:importance}).

\subsection{Study Site and Instrumentation}

The Ecological and Digital Building (EDB) is a single-story
demonstration building located on the campus of the École Polytechnique de Thiès,
Senegal (14.7957\textdegree{}N, 16.9674\textdegree{}W, 87\,m above sea
level). Its
footprint is $13.12 \times 14.10$\,m (about 185\,m$^2$), with a clear
height of 2.75\,m.

The envelope is the result of two campaigns. The walls were raised in
the 1980s in \textit{géobéton}, a compressed laterite-and-binder block,
and the \textit{typha} (\textit{Typha australis}) insulation was fitted
between 2013 and 2017 under the PNEEB-Typha
programme~\cite{niang2018}: an insulation layer retrofitted onto a
thirty-year-old earthen structure. The walls are 110\,mm thick. Neither
the order of the layers nor the thickness of the \textit{typha} was
documented, and Section~\ref{ssec:thermal} returns to the consequences.
The site lies in the Sahelian transition zone, with a rainy season
(June--October, RH 60--80\,\%, $T$ 25--32\,\textdegree{}C) and a dry
season with harmattan continental winds (November--May, RH
30--55\,\%, large diurnal amplitudes).

Instrumentation comprises a single connected sensor node (identifier
``node\_01'' in the released data) recording at a nominal 10-minute
interval. Three of its channels are used here: interior air temperature
$T_\text{int}$ (\textdegree{}C), interior relative humidity
$\text{RH}_\text{int}$ (\%), and near-envelope exterior temperature
$T_\text{ext}$ (\textdegree{}C). Timestamps are in UTC.

Interior temperature and humidity come from a DHT22 (AM2302) module
($\pm0.5$\,\textdegree{}C, $\pm2\,\%$~RH), exterior temperature from a
DS18B20 probe ($\pm0.5$\,\textdegree{}C) mounted out of direct sunlight
but without a ventilated radiation shield; no calibration or drift
check was performed. Three consequences follow. Reported errors are
measured against the sensor's own output, since a systematic offset
cancels when predicting the same instrument. The $\pm2\,\%$~RH
specification is of the same order as the margin separating the
rainy-season mean of 67\,\% from the 70\,\% comfort ceiling, so that
comparison is indicative. And $T_\text{ext}$, partly coupled to the
wall it sits beside, is a near-envelope rather than a free-field
temperature, so \sigrat compares that quantity with the interior.

Both sensors are read by an Arduino Nano 33 IoT (Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware}), which computes a heat
index on board and publishes each timestamped sample as JSON over Wi-Fi
to AWS IoT Core using MQTT over TLS; a rule forwards the message to a
Lambda function that writes it to a DynamoDB table, and the CSV exports
analysed here are dumps of that table. There is no local buffering, so
an interruption costs a sample outright, and availability is reported
as completeness (Table~\ref{tab:stats}).

\subsection{Data Consolidation and Completeness}
\label{ssec:completeness}

Two CSV exports were provided, the second a strict subset of the first;
after deduplication on the \texttt{Date} field the consolidated dataset
contains 2,237 observations spanning 16~July to 4~December 2025. Two
quality issues were addressed: 391 $T_\text{ext}$ values absent in
July, attributed to delayed sensor deployment and filled by ERA5
(Section~\ref{ssec:era5}); and a derived heat index correlated with
$T_\text{int}$ and $\text{RH}_\text{int}$ at $r>0.93$, dropped at load
so that it cannot reach any feature set. A 99-day gap
(19~August--26~November 2025) separates the two seasonal windows, which
motivates segment-aware preprocessing and precludes long-horizon
cross-season modeling.

The record is sparser than its calendar extent suggests
(Table~\ref{tab:stats}). The two windows span 41.8~days but hold only
15.5~days of data, an overall completeness of 37.2\,\%, and they are of
very unequal quality: the dry window is one uninterrupted 8.0-day block
at 90.4\,\%, the rainy window 12 disjoint fragments at 24.4\,\%, with
$T_\text{ext}$ sparser still there (16.4\,\%). After resampling to the
15-minute grid, 41.2\,\% of the interior slots and 33.5\,\% of the
$T_\text{ext}$ slots are backed by an actual sample; the remainder is
interpolated or substituted from reanalysis. All seasonal comparisons
below rest on these unequal supports, and rainy-season outages cannot
be assumed missing at random.

\subsection{ERA5 Enrichment via Open-Meteo}
\label{ssec:era5}

Missing and supplementary meteorological variables are retrieved from
the ERA5-Land reanalysis~\cite{muñozsabater2021} via the Open-Meteo
Historical Archive API~\cite{zippenfenig2023}. Seven hourly variables
are fetched at the EDB coordinates, linearly interpolated to the
15-minute pipeline step and aligned to the sensor index. Validation
against the available on-site $T_\text{ext}$ measurements yields
MAE$\,{\approx}\,1.2$\,\textdegree{}C and Pearson $r>0.92$, consistent
with published ERA5-Land accuracy in West Africa.

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture}

The pipeline is organized in six independent, testable layers
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}), implemented as Python scripts and a
batch orchestrator with fixed random seeds
(\texttt{random\_state=42}). The repository at \repourl{} contains the
two raw sensor exports, the scripts, a pinned dependency list and a
table mapping each command to the table or figure it produces; the eight
commands it lists regenerate every quantitative result in this paper on
Windows, macOS or Linux.

\subsection{CEB-Aware Feature Engineering}
\label{ssec:features}

Thirty-six predictors exploit physical knowledge of the \ceb system.
They include the current interior temperature and humidity $y(t)$:
withholding them costs 11--27\,\% of accuracy at short horizons. Lagged
exterior temperatures at
$\Delta t \in \{15\,\text{min},\,1\,\text{h},\,2\,\text{h},\,6\,\text{h}\}$
are calibrated around the measured thermal lag (${\approx}1.75$\,h),
and rolling mean and standard deviation of $T_\text{ext}$ over 1\,h and
6\,h capture the short memory of the envelope. Temporal cycles are
encoded as $(\sin,\cos)$ pairs for hour-of-day and day-of-year.
Autoregressive lags of $T_\text{int}$ and $\text{RH}_\text{int}$ at
$\Delta t \in \{15\,\text{min},\,1\,\text{h},\,24\,\text{h}\}$ anchor
predictions to recent state, and the six Open-Meteo exogenous variables
complete the set.

\subsection{Model and Validation Strategy}
\label{ssec:model}

The forecasting model is a \texttt{HistGradientBoosting\allowbreak Regressor}
(scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}), trained with quantile loss at
$\alpha \in \{0.05,\,0.50,\,0.95\}$ to produce point forecasts (median)
and 90\,\% nominal prediction intervals, with 400 estimators, learning
rate 0.05 and maximum depth~6. Validation follows a walk-forward
strategy: \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} with $k=4$ expanding folds ensures
no future information leaks into training, and four horizons are
evaluated independently (15\,min, 1\,h, 6\,h, 24\,h). Feature
importance is assessed by permutation on each walk-forward test fold
($n=189$ at $h{+}1$\,h), with 5 repeats per feature.

Two properties were verified. First, the absence of temporal leakage:
every feature at time $t$, recomputed on the series truncated at $t$,
is identical to its value on the full series, so no feature reads past
$t$. Second, the uncertainty on each reported gain: with only four
folds a mean is a weak summary, so per-sample absolute errors are
pooled across the fold test sets and resampled jointly (paired
bootstrap, 2,000 replicates) to give a 95\,\% interval on the
difference in MAE.

\subsection{Hygrothermal Overview}

Table~\ref{tab:stats} summarizes the seasonal statistics. The interior
mean temperature is 29.6\,\textdegree{}C during the rainy season and
26.7\,\textdegree{}C in the dry season, reflecting both the lower diurnal
cycle amplitude in the dry season and the thermal buffering of
the CEB mass. Interior RH drops between seasons: 67.0\,\% on
average during the rainy season against 45.9\,\% during the dry season.
The calendar heatmap (Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}) visualizes the abrupt regime
transition around late November, where cooler nights and drier air

\subsection{Thermal Signature of the CEB+Typha Envelope}
\label{ssec:thermal}

Three metrics characterize the envelope's dynamic thermal
behavior (Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal}). Damping is reported throughout as the
ratio of standard deviations \sigrat $=0.54$, computed on the 1,846
concurrent $(T_\text{int},T_\text{ext})$ samples: the interior standard
deviation, 2.50\,\textdegree{}C, is 54\,\% of the exterior value of
4.62\,\textdegree{}C. The median peak lag of $+1.75$\,h corresponds to the
delay between daily $T_\text{ext}$ and $T_\text{int}$ maxima, computed
on the 15-minute grid after a centred 1-hour rolling mean, over $n=9$
days with at least 18\,h of data (interquartile range $+1.00$ to
$+3.00$\,h, full range $+0.50$ to $+3.25$\,h). Peak picking on an
hourly grid quantises each daily estimate and inflates the median to
$+2.0$\,h; a cross-correlation estimate over the whole record
independently returns $+1.75$\,h.

The third metric is the reduction in diurnal amplitude: over the
20~days with usable daily extrema, mean interior amplitude is
5.19\,\textdegree{}C against 10.92\,\textdegree{}C outdoors, a 52.5\,\%
reduction (Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal}, panel~c). Only \sigrat is used as
the damping indicator. The all-season regression
$T_\text{int} = 0.42\,T_\text{ext} + 16.6$ gives a compact empirical
transfer function for simplified energy models.

The measured lag of $+1.75$\,h is short compared with the values usually
associated with massive earth walls, but the comparison is only
meaningful at equal thickness: for rammed earth, reported time lags
scale from 2.8\,h at 110\,mm to 6.2\,h at 220\,mm and 9.3\,h at
330\,mm~\cite{soebarto2009,fabbri2014}. The envelope here is 110\,mm
thick, and 1.75\,h sits in the same range as the 2.8\,h reported at that
thickness. Wall thinness alone therefore accounts for the short lag, and
no resistive contribution from the \textit{typha} layer needs to be
invoked to explain it. The assembly damps well, with \sigrat $=0.54$,
but its short thermal delay follows from a thin wall and is not
evidence of a resistive-capacitive hybrid.

What the data still cannot resolve is whether \textit{typha} shortens
the lag further than thinness alone would. Settling that needs the layer
build-up (which face the insulation sits on, and its thickness)
together with a wall-surface or interstitial measurement, none of which
was available.

\subsection{Hygrothermal Comfort}

Using the ASHRAE~55 tropical comfort zone for naturally ventilated
buildings (24--30\,\textdegree{}C, 30--70\,\% RH~\cite{ashrae55}),
comfort fractions differ by season: 66.9\,\% in the dry window against
17.3\,\% in the rainy one. Given the unequal supports
(Section~\ref{ssec:completeness}), these are observed fractions over
the available samples, not season-wide estimates. The rainy-season
shortfall is driven by humidity: mean $\text{RH}_\text{int}=67$\,\%
sits just below the 70\,\% ceiling, but exceedances during
precipitation push measurements outside the zone. Hygric management
(cross-ventilation, vapor-permeable finishes), more than thermal
inertia alone, is the priority lever there.

\subsection{Predictive Model Performance}

\textit{Baselines.} Persistence, $\hat{y}(t{+}h) = y(t)$, is a strong
competitor on a smooth signal. We report three baselines: persistence;
a linear autoregressive model refitted on each training fold from the
interior lags; and the seasonal naive predictor
$\hat{y}(t{+}h) = y(t{+}h{-}24\,\text{h})$. All three are scored on the
same walk-forward test folds as the model.

\textit{Increment formulation.} The model predicts the increment
$y(t{+}h) - y(t)$ and the level is reconstructed by adding $y(t)$; all
metrics are computed on the reconstructed level. Regressing the level
directly requires boosted trees to approximate $y(t{+}h) \approx y(t)$
through piecewise-constant splits, whose residual is coarser than the
0.12\,\textdegree{}C separating two consecutive samples: the level
formulation reaches only 0.477\,\textdegree{}C MAE at 15\,min against
0.094\,\textdegree{}C for the increment.

\textit{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the outcome. The
model has the lowest MAE at seven of the eight horizon/target pairs,
but not all margins are resolved. Interior temperature carries the
result: 0.09\,\textdegree{}C at 15\,min, $+22.9\,\%$ over persistence
(95\,\% CI $[+17.5,\,+27.8]$); 0.23\,\textdegree{}C at 1\,h,
$+47.7\,\%$ $[+43.2,\,+51.7]$; 0.94\,\textdegree{}C at 6\,h,
$+56.4\,\%$ $[+52.3,\,+60.3]$. Humidity behaves differently, with
$+4.9\,\%$ at 15\,min $[+0.7,\,+8.9]$ and at 1\,h $[-0.4,\,+9.9]$, the
second not clearing zero, since persistence is already close to the
sensor's noise floor on $\text{RH}_\text{int}$; the gain is resolved
again at 6\,h, $+12.1\,\%$ $[+5.8,\,+18.4]$. At 24\,h neither target is
resolved: temperature loses $4.6\,\%$ $[-10.6,\,+0.9]$ and humidity
gains $2.8\,\%$ $[-1.8,\,+7.3]$, persistence and the seasonal baseline
coinciding at that horizon. One day ahead the model and simple
repetition are indistinguishable on this record, the 99-day
interruption being the plausible cause.

\textit{Interval calibration.} Empirical coverage of the nominally
90\,\% intervals is 76\,\% and 70\,\% at 15\,min, 49\,\% and 55\,\% at
1\,h, degrading to 16--22\,\% at 24\,h. The increment formulation
improves coverage by 13 to 43 percentage points at 15\,min and 1\,h (the
level formulation gave 18--44\,\% throughout); what remains is the
known over-confidence of quantile gradient boosting on small datasets.
Conformal prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2021}, implemented as a drop-in
layer by the MAPIE library, is the priority extension and a
prerequisite to any operational use.

Per-fold results vary widely: on temperature at 15\,min the MAE
across the four expanding folds ranges from 0.12 to 0.93\,\textdegree{}C
under the level formulation. The worst fold is the first one tested
entirely in the dry season, after training almost only on rainy-season
data, which points to the discontinuity documented in
Section~\ref{ssec:completeness}.

\subsection{Feature Importance: Envelope Thermal Memory}
\label{ssec:importance}

Permutation importance is computed on the increment model of
$T_\text{int}$ at $h{+}1$\,h, with the hyperparameters of
Section~\ref{ssec:model} and squared loss: a model is fitted on the
training part of each walk-forward fold, each feature is permuted 5
times on the test fold, and the decrease in $R^2$ of the increment is
averaged over the four folds (Fig.~\ref{fig:importance}). The
hour-of-day cosine leads ($0.92 \pm 0.61$ across folds), followed by
exterior temperature six hours earlier ($0.16 \pm 0.29$) and the
hour-of-day sine ($0.044$); no other feature exceeds $0.02$.

The folds separate the two regimes. On the first test fold, whose
samples come mostly from the rainy window, exterior temperature six
hours earlier leads at $0.60$, about nine times the hour-of-day cosine;
on the three dry-season folds the hour-of-day cosine leads, at $0.91$
to $1.39$. Within the exterior-temperature family, the 6-hour lag is
followed at a distance by the 1-hour rolling standard deviation
($0.017$), the 6-hour rolling standard deviation ($0.008$) and the
6-hour rolling mean ($0.007$); the instantaneous value ranks eleventh
at $0.005$.

A six-hour scale is consistent with the low-pass response described by
the damping ratio and peak lag of Section~\ref{ssec:thermal}, and it is
recovered without physical supervision. On a regular diurnal cycle,
however, exterior temperature six hours earlier also tracks the time of
day, so this lag and the hour-of-day encoding share part of their
information; the hour-of-day cosine takes the lead on the dry-season
folds, where diurnal amplitudes are largest. A reduced-order model of
such an envelope needs outdoor temperature over the previous hours, not
only the current reading.

Permutation importance is unreliable when predictors are correlated, so
the ranking was checked two further ways. \textit{Ablation} refits the
model without a group of features and gives the 6-hour rolling mean of
exterior temperature more weight than permutation does: dropping it
alone costs $+7.9\,\%$ MAE, more than dropping all fourteen Open-Meteo
variables together ($+6.3\,\%$), and removing the temporal cycles costs
$+10.0\,\%$. \textit{Within-regime} analysis addresses the concern that
a variable separating the two windows could rank high merely as a season
indicator: recomputed inside each block, the ranking reproduces the
pattern of the folds, the 6-hour \emph{lag} leading the rainy block by a
factor of five and the hour-of-day cycle leading the eight-day dry
block.

The same tests temper the negative result on moisture. Exterior
dew-point ranks 16th of 36 over the folds, yet \emph{improves} to 6th within
the rainy block, the opposite of what a season proxy would do, and
removing the five moisture variables costs $+3.4\,\%$ MAE. The
contribution is small but not null, where hygroscopic coupling would
predict a larger role~\cite{liuzzi2018}. Whether that reflects the
physics or the limited support of the record (41.8~days at 37\,\%
completeness, the rainy season at 24\,\%) cannot be settled here.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\textit{Physical interpretation.}
The damping ratio of 0.54 falls within the 0.5--0.7 range reported for
compressed earth blocks~\cite{liuzzi2018,fabbri2014}, and the peak lag
of $+1.75$\,h at the low end of the values for earth walls, which run
from under 3\,h for thin sections to above 9\,h beyond
330\,mm~\cite{soebarto2009,soudani2017}. With a 110\,mm envelope, that
is where this building belongs: thinness accounts for the delay, and
the tempting reading of the \textit{typha} layer as a resistive element
in a resistive-capacitive hybrid is neither needed nor supported.

The pairing is nonetheless unusual. A 110\,mm earth wall would
ordinarily be a poor thermal buffer, yet the damping ratio of 0.54
matches what the literature reports for much thicker
sections~\cite{liuzzi2018,fabbri2014}. Attenuation without delay
remains to be explained, and testing it requires the layer build-up and
an interstitial measurement, neither of which was available. The
campaign contributes meanwhile a building-scale record of this specific
retrofitted assembly.

\textit{Long-horizon performance ceiling.}
The model beats all three baselines up to 6\,h but loses to persistence
on temperature at 24\,h (Table~\ref{tab:results}). This does not indicate
a failure of the model class but a structural limitation of the data: the
99-day gap between the rainy and dry seasons means no training fold
contains a continuous stretch long enough to learn a cross-season daily
cycle, so simple repetition is hard to beat one day ahead. A continuous
campaign of $\geq$6 months across at least one full seasonal transition
is the improvement most likely to lift this ceiling. Pre-trained
time-series foundation models (Chronos-T5~\cite{ansari2024}, Lag-Llama,
TimesFM) deployed in zero-shot are an alternative direction once denser
data becomes available.

\textit{Generalizability and limitations.}
The pipeline runs unchanged on Windows, macOS and Linux, requires only
freely available data, and applies to any earthen building with a
single connected sensor inside ERA5 coverage. The main limitations are
the single-sensor setup, which precludes intra-building gradients; the
temporal discontinuity; the absence of heat-flux and air-velocity
measurements; and uncalibrated instrumentation with an unshielded
exterior probe, which bounds the precision of every indicator reported
here. A second campaign should add a radiation shield and a
reference-grade co-located sensor, with a continuous record across a
full seasonal transition.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This paper presented an end-to-end open-source pipeline for the
hygrothermal characterization and probabilistic prediction of a
vernacular \ceb building in a tropical-sahelian climate. From 2,237 IoT
measurements enriched with ERA5 reanalysis, three indicators quantify
the envelope thermal inertia: \sigrat $=0.54$, a median peak lag of
$+1.75$\,h and a 52.5\,\% diurnal amplitude reduction. A
HistGradientBoosting quantile model predicting increments reaches
0.09\,\textdegree{}C MAE at 15\,min and 0.23\,\textdegree{}C at 1\,h,
improving on persistence by 22.9\,\% and 47.7\,\%. Over the walk-forward
folds, the hour-of-day cycle leads the permutation ranking and exterior
temperature six hours earlier comes first among exterior predictors; in
ablation, the 6-hour rolling mean of exterior temperature is worth
7.9\,\% of MAE, which indicates a response of the envelope to outdoor
conditions over several hours. Exterior moisture variables contribute
only marginally.

The pipeline is released at \repourl. Conformal calibration, multi-zone
instrumentation and a documented envelope build-up are the next steps
toward a standardized data-driven characterization of African earthen
construction.
